Agentic AI · Anti-money laundering · UAE

One policy, two regulators.

Can an AI agent spot where a bank's anti-money-laundering policy falls short of both of its regulators? I built four of them and put them to the test.

01 The rulebook

Every bank keeps a rulebook for stopping dirty money. It is called an anti-money-laundering (AML) policy.

What if it is quietly missing a rule?

02 What it guards against

Money laundering is how criminals make illegal money look legitimate. They place it in a bank, layer it through many accounts to hide where it came from, then pull it back out looking clean.

A bank's AML policy sets out how it spots and stops each stage.

03 The stakes

Regulators check these policies, and gaps are expensive. In 2023 Dubai's financial regulator, the DFSA, fined Mirabaud (Middle East) about USD 3 million after its systems missed suspicious patterns across linked customer accounts.

One of my real-world test cases is based on this enforcement action.

04 The UAE twist

In the UAE, a bank can answer to two regulators. The Central Bank (CBUAE) covers the mainland. The DFSA covers the DIFC, Dubai's financial free zone.

Their AML rules overlap, but they do not match. Take record keeping: at least 5 years under one, at least 6 under the other.

05 Why it's hard

So one policy can pass one regulator and fail the other. "Keep records for 5 years" is fine on the mainland and a gap in the DIFC.

Multiply that by hundreds of rules that point to each other. Checking by hand is slow and easy to get wrong.

06 Why AI, and why this study

AI language models can read regulations. But the same model can be set up in very different ways, and nobody had tested which set-up works best for this UAE problem.

07 The questions

My thesis asked two things. Can AI agents reliably find AML compliance gaps across both UAE regulators? And which agent design suits which kind of task?

08 Four agents

I built four agents on the same AI model. The only difference is how each one works through a policy.

Each keeps its colour and shape for the rest of the story.

09 How I built it

The regulations are cut into short passages and indexed by meaning, so an agent can pull up the right rule even when a policy words it differently.

An open-source model makes each call, set to its most consistent mode. Everything runs on one cloud data platform, so every result can be traced and repeated.

10 The test

300 policies written from the real rulebooks. Half hide a gap; half are fully compliant. They are split across five levels, from a single rule up to both regulators at once.

Ten more cases are based on real DFSA enforcement actions.

11 How to score it

Two questions matter. Of the real gaps, how many did the agent catch? That is recall. Of the alarms it raised, how many were real? That is precision.

A useful agent needs both. F1 combines them into one score.

12 The twist, part 1

The RAG agent caught almost every gap: recall between 93% and 100% at all five levels.

Looks perfect.

13 The twist, part 2

Now add precision. About half of RAG's alarms were false. It flagged nearly every policy, compliant or not.

A rule that simply says "gap" every time lands in the same spot. Near-perfect recall was a warning sign.

14 Who did best

Plan-and-Execute was the most precise agent at all five levels and had the highest average F1: 0.673, against 0.662 for RAG and 0.537 for ReAct.

But the error bars overlap, and only 2 of 15 head-to-head comparisons were statistically significant. These are consistent tendencies, not proof.

15 Real-world cases

On the 10 cases based on real enforcement actions, the ranking changed. ReAct scored best (F1 0.769). Plan-and-Execute was the most precise, but it missed the most severe case.

Ten cases is a small sample, so this is a signal to test further.

16 The verdict

No single design wins. The right agent depends on the job you give it.

17 Why a bank should care

Every false alarm costs an analyst's time. On the single-rule DFSA level, RAG raised 57 alarms for 30 real gaps; 29 were false. Plan-and-Execute raised 35, with 13 false, but missed 8 real gaps where RAG missed 2.

That trade-off is a business decision. A person should make the final call.

18 What this isn't, yet

This is a first controlled comparison, not a finished product. The limits are on the board, and so is what I would build next.
